The Memo — Welcome Email Sequence

3-email onboarding series for new subscribers | Draft v1 | March 31, 2026

Sequence Strategy

Why This Matters

32.3% of Fluent-acquired subscribers (and 14.7% of organic) unsubscribe within 30 days. The welcome sequence is the single highest-leverage intervention for converting new signups into active, retained openers. Axios Sports found that a personal welcome email from the editor drove a 6% lift in overall open rates. This sequence adapts that principle for The Memo.

Narrative Arc

The sequence moves from personal connection (Email 1: "here's a human behind this newsletter") → value demonstration (Email 2: "here's proof of what you'll get") → habit formation (Email 3: "you're part of something — reply and tell us what you want").

Re-Engagement Sequence: Saving At-Risk Subscribers Before Force Churn

The Problem

Currently, subscribers who stop engaging eventually get removed through force churn in batch purges (~10K every 1–2 weeks). This is reactive — by the time a subscriber is purged, they've been inactive for weeks or months. A re-engagement sequence intercepts them at the point where they've gone quiet but haven't mentally "quit" yet. Industry benchmarks suggest re-engagement flows can recover 3–8% of lapsed subscribers.

Sequence Architecture

The re-engagement flow is a 3-email series that triggers based on inactivity milestones. Each email escalates in urgency. Subscribers who re-engage at any point exit the flow and return to regular sends. Those who don't are moved to a suppression list and eventually force-churned.

When to Trigger

MilestoneActionRationale
21 days of no opensEnter re-engagement flow → Email R13 weeks without an open across AM + PM sends = ~30+ missed emails. This is the sweet spot — early enough that they might remember signing up, late enough to confirm it's a pattern, not a vacation.
35 days of no opensEmail R2 (if R1 not opened)5 weeks. Urgency increases. Still recoverable but window is closing.
50 days of no opensEmail R3 — final attemptLast chance before suppression. Direct, honest, low-friction.
60 days of no opensMove to suppression list → stop all sendsContinuing to send to confirmed non-openers damages Gmail sender reputation. Suppress, don't delete — they can re-subscribe later.

Why This Works

Loss aversion: "We're about to remove you" is more motivating than "We miss you." People respond to the threat of losing something more than the appeal of gaining something.

Single CTA: One button. No stories, no content, no distractions. The entire email is engineered to get one click — which re-engages them and sends a strong positive signal to Gmail.

Respectful exit: Telling someone you'll remove them (rather than asking them to unsubscribe) is a mark of a quality newsletter. It mirrors The Athletic's philosophy of preferring a sold-out 20K-seater over a half-filled 80K-seater. Subscribers who stay after this email are genuinely engaged.

Implementation Notes

Defining "No Opens"

Use unique opens as the trigger metric. A subscriber who opens any Memo send (AM, PM, or weekend) resets their inactivity clock to zero. The 21/35/50 day thresholds mean they've missed roughly 30+/50+/70+ individual sends without opening a single one.

Suppression vs. Deletion

ActionWhat HappensWhy
Suppress (recommended)Stop all sends. Keep subscriber record. They can re-subscribe via the website.Preserves the relationship. They may come back during a tentpole event (Super Bowl, March Madness, NFL Draft). Doesn't inflate unsub metrics.
DeleteRemove from list entirely.Only if list size is causing cost/deliverability issues. Counts as churn in reporting.

"Keep Sending" Button Mechanics

The Email R3 CTA ("Yes — keep sending me The Memo") should link to a simple landing page or trigger that: (1) marks the subscriber as re-engaged, (2) resets their inactivity clock, and (3) optionally shows a "Welcome back" confirmation page with the Gmail Primary tab instructions. This is a second chance to fix deliverability for subscribers who were in Promotions the whole time.

Expected Impact

Modeling the Recovery

If the current force churn removes ~10K subscribers every 1–2 weeks, and this re-engagement flow recovers even 5% of those before they hit force churn, that's 500–1,000 subscribers preserved per purge cycle — roughly 1,000–2,000 per month. At a 61% open rate, that's an additional 600–1,200 opens per send. It's not a silver bullet, but it compounds month over month and costs nothing but a few hours of setup.
